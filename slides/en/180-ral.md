## RAL

#### *Unit 9 · optional · the register stops being an address*

- Every chip has configuration registers, and a small SoC has **two hundred**
- Without RAL, the testbench writes them like this: `addr = 8'h04`, `wdata = ...`, and the
  scoreboard keeps a `logic [31:0]` for each one
- The day the architect moves `SCRATCH` from `0x04` to `0x24`, you have to look for
  `8'h04` all over the testbench — and `0x04` was also the value of something else
- **RAL** —*Register Abstraction Layer*— is the layer that puts a name on that:
  `model.SCRATCH.write(status, 'h1000_0000)`
- It is optional and it goes at the end on purpose: **it is only understood once you have
  written the testbench it replaces**

Note:
This unit is short and it is the last one, and both things are deliberate. RAL is the
piece of UVM most often taught without context —there are courses that give it on day
2— and that way it is not understood: it looks like one more API. With the capstone just done,
on the other hand, the student has the comparison inside their head. They wrote the
scoreboard that predicts `CTRL`; now I show them the library that does it on its own,
and also where it does not.
The number of 200 registers is not rhetorical. A PHY block or a memory
controller has that many, and the register spec arrives as a spreadsheet. The
next step, which this course does not take, is **generating the model** from that spreadsheet
with IP-XACT or SystemRDL: nobody writes 200 registers by hand. It is worth saying, because
otherwise the student walks away with the idea that RAL is a lot of typing.
And the argument about moving `0x04` to `0x24` is the one that convinces a
sceptic: it is not elegance, it is that the map gets written **only once**.

## RAL

#### *Three pieces, and only one knows what bus is underneath*

| Piece | What it is | How many times it gets written |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `uvm_reg_block` | the map: registers, fields, addresses | one per **DUT** |
| `uvm_reg_adapter` | translates access ↔ bus transaction | one per **protocol** |
| `uvm_reg_predictor` | what the monitor saw, said in registers | none: it comes done |

- The model **does not know** there is an APB underneath. You change the adapter and the same
  model drives AHB
- The adapter is the file that comes with a VIP you bought. It is twenty lines
- And underneath everything, without a single change: the driver, the monitor and the agent of the
  capstone

Note:
The table is the slide. That the third column says *one per DUT*, *one per
protocol* and *none* is the whole economic argument for RAL, and it is the one that
holds the decision up in a project meeting.
It is worth pointing at the last line with a finger: nothing the student wrote yesterday gets
replaced here. `code/u9/ral/` compiles the interface, the transaction,
the driver, the monitor and the agent of `d7-final/solucion/` exactly as they are, through
`+incdir`. RAL is a layer, not a different testbench — and it is the reason why
this unit fits into three files.
If somebody asks about the backdoor: it exists, it gets declared with `add_hdl_path` and it reads
the signal of the RTL without going through the bus. It does not go into the course because it depends on the
hierarchy support of the simulator, which is precisely what changes between
tools. The frontdoor —the one that uses the real bus— is the one you have to know.

## RAL

#### *The model: the table of the spec, in code*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/apb_reg_block.svh|lines=12-36}}

- A field gets declared with `configure()`, and the argument that matters is the
  **access string**: `"RW"`, `"RO"`, `"WOC"`…
- That string **is the specification**. It is not documentation: it is what UVM uses
  to predict, and to generate the tests of the last slide

Note:
`configure(parent, size, lsb_pos, access, volatile, reset, has_reset, is_rand,
individually_accessible)` — nine positional arguments, and it is the ugliest signature
in UVM. It is worth writing it on the blackboard once; afterwards the student copies it from the
example, which is what everybody does.
`CLR` is the field that justifies the slide. The spec of the capstone says *"writing a
1 puts `ACC` and `OVF` at zero, and the bit always reads 0"*, and that is trap 2
of the small print — the one that makes the hand-written scoreboard fail. UVM has an
access that says exactly that and it is called **`WOC`**: on the write the mirror
goes down to zero, and the read side of the field is not used to predict anything.
The complete list of accesses is in the LRM 1800.2, and there are twenty-five. You do not have to
know them by heart: you have to know that **the list exists**, and look there before
inventing a `set_compare` or a callback. Half of the bad register models
you come across are an `RW` field with patches on top.

## RAL

#### *The adapter: the only thing that knows there is an APB*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/apb_reg_adapter.svh|lines=16-37}}

- `reg2bus` goes down: the model asks for a generic access, this builds the transaction
  of the bus
- `bus2reg` goes up: the transaction comes back —from the driver, or **from the monitor**— and this
  counts it in terms of an access
- `PSLVERR` turns into `UVM_NOT_OK`, and there the `status` of every `write()`
  stops being decorative

Note:
Twenty-two lines, and that is the whole file. It is worth saying the number out loud,
because RAL has a reputation for being heavy and the project-specific part is
this one.
The `reg2bus` / `bus2reg` symmetry is the one to fix: one function per
direction, and neither of the two has state. The `uvm_reg_bus_op` is the common
currency —`kind`, `addr`, `data`, `status`— and it is deliberately as flat as possible:
any register bus fits in there.
The `const ref` of the `reg2bus` comes from the signature of the base class and has to be copied
exactly. It works on Verilator 5.052, which was not obvious.
And a warning from the field: `bus2reg` is called **by the predictor too**, with the
item the monitor saw. An adapter that reads something from the driver —or that depends on
having built the transaction itself— works with automatic prediction and breaks
the day you switch to explicit. That is why both functions are pure.

## RAL

#### *Hooking it up: three lines and a `connect_phase`*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/ral_base_test.svh|lines=40-50}}

- `set_sequencer` is what makes `model.CTRL.write()` end up in the driver:
  the model sends items through **the sequencer of the agent that already existed**
- `set_auto_predict(0)` switches off the automatic prediction, and the `uvm_reg_predictor`
  replaces it by listening to the **analysis port of the monitor**
- And the `model.build()` of the `build_phase`: a `uvm_reg_block` is a `uvm_object`,
  not a component. **Nobody calls it for you**

Note:
The phase trap that takes everybody's first afternoon is the last
line of the slide. `apb_reg_block::type_id::create("model")` gives back an object
with zero registers, because `build()` here is an ordinary method that gets called by hand
—not the `build_phase` of a component, which UVM walks on its own—. The symptom is a
`uvm_fatal` from `uvm_reg_map` that talks about a null register and does not mention the
word `build`.
The second thing to point out is where the sequencer comes from: it is
`agent_h.sequencer_h`, the same one from the capstone, the same one the normal sequences
run on. RAL does not bring a sequencer of its own. That means a register
sequence and a traffic one compete for the same driver, and there the
priorities show up — which is a topic for another course, but it is worth them knowing it exists.

## RAL

#### *Prediction: who tells the model what happened*

| | Who predicts | What happens if the DUT does something else |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **automatic** | the `write()` call itself | the model **does not find out**: it believes what it meant to send |
| **explicit** | the `uvm_reg_predictor`, from the monitor | the model believes **what happened on the wire** |

- The automatic one is one line fewer and it is the one that comes up in the tutorials
- The explicit one is the one to use, and for the same reason the scoreboard
  of the capstone looks at the monitor and not at the driver
- And it is free: the monitor and its analysis port **were already there**

Note:
This is the judgement slide of the unit and it is worth not rushing it. With
`set_auto_predict(1)`, `model.CTRL.write(status, 'h1)` updates the mirror at the
moment it gets called, before the bus has done anything. If the driver sends
the transfer wrong, or the DUT ignores it, the model goes on being convinced that
`EN=1`. It is exactly the design mistake the course has been attacking since day 1: **the
one who checks cannot believe the one who drives**.
With explicit prediction the path is the long and correct one: the driver drives,
the monitor sees the wire, the analysis port publishes it, the predictor calls
`bus2reg` and only then does the mirror change. It is the same drawing as the scoreboard, with
a piece of the library instead of a hand-written class.
The bonus, which can be seen in the example: since the predictor listens to the bus and not to the
calls, the model also finds out about the transfers **somebody else** made —
another sequence, another master, an ordinary module. Automatic prediction cannot
do that even in principle.

## RAL

#### *The stimulus, without a single `PADDR`*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/ral_test.svh|lines=21-34}}

- There is no address in any call: the address is **in the map**
- Nobody assigned the mirror. `get_mirrored_value()` gives back 1 because the monitor saw
  the transfer and the predictor counted it
- Underneath, the driver of the capstone is doing SETUP, ACCESS and the *wait state* of
  the read, the same as yesterday

`bash run.sh` · `code/u9/ral/`
<!-- .element: class="comando" -->

Note:
It is worth running it live and looking at the log with `grep MONITOR`: for every line of
stimulus there is a complete APB transfer, with its address and its handshake,
done by the driver they wrote. It is the demonstration that RAL does not
replace the testbench, it drives it.
The line most worth discussing is `model.CTRL.read(status, data)` against
`model.CTRL.mirror(status, UVM_CHECK)`. Both read from the DUT. The difference is
that `mirror` compares against what the model believed **before** the read, and if
it does not match it reports a `uvm_error` without anybody writing a check. It is a
register scoreboard in one word.
And the third form, which is not on the slide and is worth naming: `set()` +
`update()`. `set()` changes the desired value in the model, without touching the bus, and
`update()` writes **only the registers whose desired value differs from the mirror**.
It is how a whole block gets configured at the start of a test, and it is where RAL stops
being convenient and starts genuinely saving simulation time.

## RAL

#### *The tests you did not write*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/builtin_test.svh|lines=29-35}}

- Both come from `uvm-core`. **They know nothing about this DUT**: they read the model and
  generate the stimulus and the check out of it
- `uvm_reg_hw_reset_seq` reads everything after the reset and compares it with the reset
  value declared
- `uvm_reg_bit_bash_seq` writes and reads **every writable bit of every register**,
  one by one, in both directions

Note:
This pair of sequences is the RAL argument that wins discussions, because it does not
appeal to taste: they are two real tests, free, that on a block of 200 registers
cover 80 % of the bugs of address decoding and of fields wired the wrong way round.
There are more in the library and it is worth naming them: `uvm_reg_access_seq` (writes through
the frontdoor and reads through the backdoor, and the other way round), `uvm_reg_shared_access_seq` for
registers in more than one map, and `uvm_mem_walk_seq` for memories. The ones that
need a backdoor stay out of the course for what was said before.
The implementation detail worth telling, because it explains why the
model has to be right: `bit_bash` **skips** the read-only fields and
the ones that are not individually accessible, and it uses the access string to know
what to expect back. Which means the quality of the test they give you is
exactly the quality of the model you wrote.

## RAL

#### *And that is why the model is the spec*

```
    CTRL.CLR as "WOC"  0 UVM_ERROR
    CTRL.CLR as "RW"   2 UVM_ERROR, from uvm_reg_bit_bash_seq
    > Writing a 1 in bit #1 of register "model.CTRL" ... yielded 'h0
      instead of 'h2
```

- One word changed in a string, and a sequence nobody wrote catches it
  with the number of the bit
- It is **trap 2** of the small print of the capstone —`CLR` never reads as 1— and
  there a whole scoreboard was needed to see it
- The register model is the only document of a project that is the spec **and**
  the code at the same time

`bash run.sh` runs both versions and compares them
<!-- .element: class="comando" -->

Note:
This is the slide that justifies the unit, and the `run.sh` of the example produces it
on its own: it runs `builtin_test` with the model right and with `+MAL`, which declares `CLR`
as `RW`, and shows the two numbers side by side.
The punchline to say out loud: in the capstone, that same trap cost writing
a scoreboard, modelling the autoclear by hand and discovering the bug with a message
of your own. Here it costs **picking one word well**, and the error message is written by
Accellera, with the number of the bit inside.
The honest downside, which has to be said so it does not sound like a brochure: if you
get it wrong the other way round —you declare `RW` a field the DUT implemented as `RW`
when the spec said something else— the model and the DUT agree on the
mistake and no sequence says anything. The register model verifies the DUT
against **the spec written in the model**, not against the real spec. That is why the
review of the model against the document is still human work, and that is why
stage 1 of the exercise prints the map so it can be compared by hand.

## RAL

#### *What RAL does not do, and it has to be said*

{{code:code/u9/ral/tb_classes/apb_reg_block.svh|lines=141-143}}

- `ACC` and `STATUS` have an address, but **they are not registers**: their value gets
  produced by a write to *another* address
- A model predicts *"what I wrote is what I am going to read"*, and for these two that
  sentence is false. `set_compare(UVM_NO_CHECK)` says so in the model
- The accumulator goes on being checked where it already was: **in the scoreboard**, which is
  the one that knows how to add
- The read updates the mirror all the same — a read *is* a prediction

Note:
If they take a single idea away from this unit, let it be this one: **an address is not a
register**. RAL models addressable storage, and it does it very well. It does not
model behaviour. The accumulator of the capstone —which adds only with `EN=1`, which
overflows, which gets cleared with `CLR`— is behaviour, and that lives in the
scoreboard, as it lived yesterday.
The failure mode of not saying it is the worst of all and it is an everyday one: the team puts
RAL in, switches on `mirror(UVM_CHECK)` over the whole map, and the regression fills up with
errors on the status registers. Since they are false positives, somebody switches the
check off **entirely** — and from then on the model verifies nothing and nobody
finds out. The difference between switching off three named fields and switching off the global
check is the whole difference.
The two `UVM_WARNING` the example prints —`GET_MIRRORED_VAL/VOL`— are the
library saying the same thing on its own account: the mirror of a `volatile` field is not
evidence. They are in the output on purpose.

## RAL

#### *Summary of the unit*

- The **model** is the map of the spec in code, and the access string of each
  field is the part that verifies
- The **adapter** is the only thing that knows about the bus: twenty lines, once per
  protocol
- The prediction goes **through the monitor**, not through the call — the same rule as the
  scoreboard
- It gives you `hw_reset_seq` and `bit_bash_seq` for free, and it charges you knowing **what is not a
  register**
- And all of this runs on **Verilator 5.052**, which until today nobody had shown

Note:
The close of the unit and of the technical course. The last line is not marketing: when
writing this unit it was first tested whether `uvm_reg_adapter` and the frontdoor
worked on Verilator, before writing a single slide, and the result is
measured in `docs/verilator.md`. Everything the unit uses works: the model, the
adapter, the `uvm_reg_predictor`, `mirror(UVM_CHECK)` and the two sequences of the
library.
For whoever goes on alone, the order of what is missing: `uvm_reg_access_seq` with
a backdoor, generating the model from IP-XACT or SystemRDL —which is how it
is really done—, and `uvm_mem` for memories. All three are extensions of what
they saw here, not new topics.
And the bridge to the exercise: the first thing they are going to be asked for in a project with
registers is exactly this, with the spreadsheet of the spec on the left-hand side of
the screen.
